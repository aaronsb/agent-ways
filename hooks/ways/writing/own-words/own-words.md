# The User's Own Words

## Their text is the gold standard

- Keep their wording, terms, structure, and thesis. If prose is needed, join their bullets into sentences in their voice.
- Never draft, rewrite, or polish prose they are writing themselves, and never invent their rewrites.
- Do not let source statistics drown out their take.
- Re-read the file right before every edit. The user may edit it between turns, and those edits must survive.
- A "content here" placeholder only marks where to reorganize. Copy transcripts and slide language verbatim. When versions conflict, ask which one is right.
- For a new page or a recurring artifact, match the style, markdown, depth, and brevity of the last approved sibling, such as last week's update. Do not invent a new format.

## Line edits and feedback lists

- For Remove, Replace, Move, and Bold edits, apply each one exactly as worded. Change nothing else, and stay inside the named file or repo.
- In a feedback list, every bullet is required, and later additions fold in. Keep what they said they love, report back item by item, and never redo approved parts.
- If they dislike an element, remove or simplify it instead of polishing it. Delete all of it, including setup lines and registrations, and add a check that keeps it out.

## Copy-edit passes

Copy edits often arrive in rapid rounds, each followed by a commit. Apply each edit exactly as given. Then sweep the whole document, because flagged examples show a pattern, not the full list. Add missing commas, quotes around terms, and light bold or italics for scanning. Remove self-aware meta language such as "this entry". Keep the voice.

## Dictation

- Capture their exact diction and main idea. Make only small accuracy edits, and list each one so they can veto it.
- In conceptual writing, label the passages, such as conventional definition, working definition, thesis, and personal grounding. State the core argument up front and again at each juncture.
- In a concept session, take one topic per turn, write up their dump, and end with "Next: X. Dump when ready."

## Long-form

- For articles and essays, write flowing narrative in their voice. Use a few headers and few bullets, avoid strings of short punchy sentences, use real quotes, and hit the target length.
- When condensing to a length, keep the strongest passages. Do not cut the parts they care most about.
- For synthesis such as READMEs and event wrap-ups, form an opinion from the team's own experience. Keep it short and link to deeper docs instead of recapping notes.
- Match the platform register the user asks for. For example, a user may want social posts to read like a casual personal update, not thought leadership.
